Step 5: Moot Court Arena
Concrete Illustrative Example: End-to-End Case Evaluation Walkthrough Click to expand / collapse walkthrough
Case Incident Description:

An eyewitness stood at a bus stop at dusk when a robbery occurred 15 meters away under low street lighting (~10 lux). The perpetrator pointed a handgun at the victim for 10 seconds before fleeing. Two weeks later, the witness identified the suspect in a 6-person photographic lineup.

Step 1: Formulate the Two Competing Scenarios (Hypotheses)
Primary Hypothesis (H₁): The eyewitness identification is accurate (The suspect is the actual perpetrator seen by the witness).
Alternative Hypothesis (H₂): The eyewitness identification is mistaken (The suspect is an innocent person mistakenly identified).
Steps 2 & 3: Factor & 3-Pillar Scrutiny

Key Factor: Weapon Focus & Distance at Dusk (Estimator variable; Importance: 8/10, decreases validity).

• Replicability (4.5/5): Robustly replicated across meta-analyses (Fawcett et al., 2013).

• Generalizability (3.5/5): Laboratory video paradigms show larger effect sizes, but 15m distance + 10 lux is a recognized empirical threshold.

• Practical Relevance (4.0/5): Perceptual degradation exceeds SESOI thresholds under combined weapon presence and dusk lighting.

Step 4: Likelihood Ratio & Phrasing

Conditional Probabilities:
• P(Evidence | H₁) = 0.30  |  • P(Evidence | H₂) = 0.65

LR = 0.30 / 0.65 = 0.46 (Anecdotal support for H₂, Jarosz & Wiley scale).

"The witness's limited visual encoding is approximately 2.17 times more likely under H₂ (mistaken identification) than under H₁ (accurate identification), providing anecdotal evidence for H₂."
Step 5 Court Defense Boundary: As an expert witness, you evaluate the likelihood of the evidence given each hypothesis (P(E|H)). Never state posterior guilt probabilities (e.g. "The suspect is 68% innocent") to avoid the Prosecutor's Fallacy!
